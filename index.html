1. What Is Phishing?

Phishing is a cyberattack in which criminals impersonate trusted people or organizations to steal passwords, financial information, or other sensitive data. Attacks can arrive through emails, text messages, phone calls, and websites.

Remember: A familiar logo or professional-looking message does not prove that it is genuine.

2. How to Identify Suspicious Emails

  • Check the full sender address for misspellings or unusual domains.
  • Be cautious of urgent threats or unexpected prizes.
  • Do not open unexpected attachments.
  • Inspect links before opening them.
  • Be suspicious of requests for passwords, payment, or verification codes.
  • Remember that good grammar does not guarantee safety.
Training example: Suspicious account message

Subject: URGENT! Your account will be closed today.

Dear User, unusual activity has been detected. Verify your account immediately to avoid suspension.

Warning signs: Generic greeting, pressure to act quickly, and a request to verify an account through a message.

This is a fictional example for awareness training.

3. Identify Fake Websites and Login Pages

  • Read the actual domain name carefully.
  • Look for misspelled brand names and extra words in domains.
  • Do not trust a site just because it displays a padlock or HTTPS.
  • Be suspicious if a login page appears after an unexpected message.
  • Navigate to the official site using a saved bookmark or a known address.
  • Never enter real credentials on a website you cannot verify.

Illustrative comparison

Expected domain: example.com

Suspicious lookalike: example-login.invalid

The second address is an illustration, not a real login service. Always verify the domain belonging to the actual organization.

4. Common Social Engineering Techniques

  • Urgency: "Act now or your account will be blocked."
  • Authority: An attacker pretends to be a manager or IT employee.
  • Fear: A message threatens a fine or account suspension.
  • Curiosity: An unexpected document or sensational link tempts you to open it.
  • Rewards: A fake prize or job offer asks for personal information.
  • Impersonation: A criminal pretends to be a friend, colleague, bank, or supplier.

Safe response: Pause, verify the request using a separate trusted channel, and report suspicious messages.

5. Practical Security Tips

  • Use unique passwords and a reputable password manager.
  • Enable multifactor authentication (MFA), preferably phishing-resistant MFA where available.
  • Keep your operating system, browser, and apps updated.
  • Never share passwords or one-time verification codes.
  • Confirm unexpected payment requests with the person using a known phone number.
  • Report suspicious messages to your email provider or IT/security team.
  • If you entered credentials on a suspicious site, change them immediately from a trusted device and contact your IT team or service provider.

6. Real-Life Case Studies

Case Study A: Stolen login credentials

In a case described by the FBI in April 2021, a criminal used a phishing email and a convincing fake website to obtain a company executive's login information. The stolen access was then used in a business email compromise scheme involving fraudulent wire transfers.

Lesson: Verify login requests and payment instructions independently, even when a message looks convincing.

Source: FBI case study

Case Study B: Fake supplier payment request

Business email compromise scams have involved criminals posing as legitimate suppliers and sending changed payment instructions. A business may transfer money to the criminal instead of its real supplier.

Lesson: Confirm bank-account changes using a previously verified phone number, not the contact details in the suspicious email.

Source: FBI BEC guidance
